An Open Letter to Watch Tower Bible and Tract Society

On the lawful use of your publications, the archives that have been pressured out of existence, and the record you are still quietly editing.

To: Watch Tower Bible and Tract Society of Pennsylvania; Watchtower Bible and Tract Society of New York, Inc.; and the Christian Congregation of Jehovah’s Witnesses.
From: the operator of Horkos.
Subject: the basis on which this archive is maintained.

This page states a position. It is not legal advice, and its author is not a lawyer. It sets out why the operator of this site believes its use of Watchtower material is permitted under United States copyright law, and invites you to say plainly, and specifically, if you disagree.

If you believe a particular entry on this site exceeds fair use, identify the entry and the passage. It will be reviewed on the merits and, if the objection is sound, corrected or removed. What follows is an argument, not a refusal to listen.

I. What this site actually does

Precision matters here, because most of the legal argument turns on it. Horkos is not a library of your publications. It does not host your books, your magazines, your PDFs or your EPUBs. It does not mirror the Watchtower Online Library, and it does not attempt to open the encrypted publication files distributed through JW Library — those are left untouched, deliberately, and every word of text on this site is taken from your own public web rendering.

What Horkos does is narrower. It checks your English-language publications against your own catalogue on a schedule. When a publication is revised, it compares the new text against the version stored previously and records which sentences changed. It then publishes the before and after of that specific passage — capped at 1,200 characters, shortened with a marker where it runs longer — alongside a note on what the alteration appears to do, and a link to your current official page so any reader can check the comparison against the source.

The quoted material is therefore the evidence of the claim, and nothing beyond it. A paragraph is reproduced in order to demonstrate that the paragraph was rewritten. That is the whole of the use.

II. The legal basis

United States copyright law does not grant an author control over every use of a work. Section 107 of the Copyright Act provides that the fair use of a copyrighted work “for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching… scholarship, or research, is not an infringement of copyright.”12 Documenting that a religious publisher altered its own published teaching, and quoting the two versions so the reader can see the alteration, sits at the centre of that list, not at its edge.

The statute directs courts to weigh four factors.12 Taking them in order, as they apply to this site:

1. The purpose and character of the use

Horkos is non-commercial. It sells nothing, carries no advertising, and solicits no donations. More importantly, its purpose is not the purpose your publications were written to serve. Your paragraph on a doctrinal requirement was written to instruct. The same paragraph reproduced here is not instruction; it is an exhibit. It appears beside its replacement, under a heading recording the date the wording changed. That recontextualisation — using an existing work as evidence about the work itself — is the kind of transformation the Supreme Court described in Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music: a use that adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.14

2. The nature of the copyrighted work

Every work quoted here is published — published, in fact, on the broadest possible scale, distributed without charge and pressed on strangers at their front doors. Nothing on this site is drawn from leaked or unpublished material. That distinction is not a small one: the strongest copyright cases against critics involve unpublished work, and this site has none.

Further, much of what is documented here is not protectable expression at all. That a date was taught, that a requirement was set at one year and is now six months, that a sentence claiming one thing has been replaced by a sentence claiming another — these are facts about your publications. Copyright protects the expression, never the facts or the ideas. The core assertion of this site is a factual one, and no copyright reaches it.

3. The amount and substantiality of the portion used

This is where Horkos differs sharply from a general archive, and the difference is intentional. Only the altered passage is published, and it is truncated at 1,200 characters. A reader cannot reconstruct an article from this site, let alone a book. There is no entry point here for someone who wants to read The Watchtower; there is only an entry point for someone who wants to know what a given paragraph used to say.

4. The effect on the potential market for the work

There is no market. You have not sold your literature since 1 March 1990, when — weeks after the Supreme Court held in Jimmy Swaggart Ministries that religious publishers were not exempt from sales tax — distribution moved to a donation-only basis.19 Your publications are given away at no charge, in print and online, worldwide. Horkos cannot undercut a price that does not exist, and cannot divert a sale that is never made.

As for the specific material this site preserves — the superseded wording — there is not merely no market for it; you have removed it from circulation yourself. A copyright holder cannot claim harm to the market for a version it has deliberately withdrawn and declines to supply. What is being protected in that scenario is not a market. It is an absence.

Where the law does not favour a general archive

Candour is worth more here than advocacy, so this should be said plainly. Courts have not been kind to projects that reproduce whole works. In Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive the Second Circuit held in 2024 that scanning books in full and lending them out was not fair use, even by a non-profit, even one copy at a time.16 And on 21 July 2026, a judge of the Southern District of New York declined to quash your subpoena aimed at the operator of JWS Library, reasoning that while the site’s analytical tools looked transformative, the full-text archive behind them did not — and observing that the operator “could have disclosed only parts of the works they directly compared or critiqued.”8

Horkos discloses only the parts it directly compares and critiques. That is not a reaction to the ruling; it is how the site was built. The line the court drew in July is the line this site already sits on, and it is the reason the argument here is made with some confidence.

III. What has happened to the others

The reason this letter exists at all is that the pattern is now well documented, and it is not a pattern of copyright enforcement.

Since 2017 you have filed roughly seventy-two subpoenas under section 512(h) of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, the provision that lets a copyright holder demand a service provider identify an anonymous user.1 What is striking is what almost never follows. Public Citizen’s Paul Alan Levy, who has litigated against several of them, records that these subpoenas have essentially never been converted into actual infringement lawsuits.1 A subpoena campaign that identifies critics and then declines to sue them is not a campaign about infringement. It is a campaign about identification — and, given that the people identified are members and former members subject to disfellowshipping and shunning, about what identification costs them.

The specific cases bear this out:

  • Kevin McFree. A former member posted a thirteen minute video commenting on excerpts of your material. You subpoenaed YouTube to unmask him. The court quashed the subpoena on fair use grounds, analysing the four factors and finding for him.12 You then sued anyway — and in May 2022, after the court signalled the claims would fail, dismissed the case with prejudice.3 Along the way, as his counsel noted, the discovery you sought had drifted to how he obtained the material and whether your systems had been accessed — questions about the source of a leak, not about copyright.3
  • The Reddit user. Another anonymous critic, defended by the Electronic Frontier Foundation. That subpoena, too, was resisted on fair use and anonymity grounds.4
  • FaithLeaks. The Truth & Transparency Foundation settled with you in July 2020 for $15,000, agreed to remove your material and never publish it again. Their public statement is the most honest description of how this works: they maintained they had not infringed anything, but “without the funds to make our arguments in court, we had no choice but to settle.”520 The merits were never reached. They were not meant to be.
  • JWS Library. In 2026 you served DMCA subpoenas on both Google and Cloudflare to unmask its operator — a current Witness who had built tools to analyse how your published statements have changed over time, and an archive so that others could verify the findings.67 The EFF moved to quash on 9 March 2026.6 On 21 July the court declined.8 The site itself now serves a single sentence: “This site has been taken offline out of an abundance of caution while we review relevant matters.”9

One clarification, in the interest of not overstating the case: watchtowerwayback.org was still operating when this letter was written. It is named here only as an archive of the kind now plainly at risk, not as one you have taken down. If that changes, this paragraph will change with it.

Courts have seen this shape before, and have named it. In Online Policy Group v. Diebold, a voting-machine company used copyright notices to suppress internal emails discussing flaws in its machines. The court held the company liable under section 512(f) for knowing misrepresentation, finding that “no reasonable copyright holder could have believed” the passages were protected, and noting that the company had never actually sued anyone — which suggested it was using the DMCA as a sword to suppress embarrassing material rather than as a shield to protect intellectual property.1013 In Religious Technology Center v. Lerma, Judge Leonie Brinkema found that although the complaint was pleaded in copyright and trade secret, “the primary motivation of RTC in suing Lerma, DGS and The Post is to stifle criticism of Scientology in general and to harass its critics.”11 And under Lenz v. Universal Music, a copyright holder must actually consider whether a use is fair before demanding its removal; failing to do so is not a technicality but a basis for liability.15

The parallels are not offered as an accusation of bad faith. They are offered because the conduct is objectively similar, the precedents are adverse, and you are on notice of both.

IV. Who paid for these publications

Not one page of the material this site quotes was produced with money that came from outside your congregations. The presses, the paper, the translation departments and the branch facilities were funded by Witnesses — by the contributions of people who put money in a box at a Kingdom Hall, and who then carried the resulting literature from door to door on their own time and at their own expense.

They were told that this work was the most important work being done on earth. Many arranged their entire lives around it: forgoing higher education, forgoing careers, forgoing relationships with family who left. Some accepted the loss of a child, or their own life, on the strength of a teaching printed in one of these publications — a teaching that was later revised without acknowledgement.

Whatever the legal position on the copyright, the moral position on the material is not seriously arguable. You do not have the standing to suppress the record of what you told those people to believe. They paid for it. They preached it. They obeyed it. Some of them died holding to it. The idea that the text of what they were taught is a proprietary asset, to be withdrawn from them at the publisher’s convenience, is an insult to what it cost them.

V. You built the law you are now using against your own people

This is the part that should give you pause, because the irony is not rhetorical. It is a matter of the reports.

Jehovah’s Witnesses did more than almost any group in American history to establish the First Amendment’s protection of unpopular publishing. Lovell v. City of Griffin (1938) established that a permit requirement for distributing literature was unconstitutional. Cantwell v. Connecticut (1940) applied the Free Exercise Clause to the states. Murdock v. Pennsylvania and West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette (both 1943) struck down a tax on evangelism and a compulsory flag salute. Your own publications rightly treat these as a legacy.

Two of them are directly on point here. In Martin v. City of Struthers (1943), decided for a Witness who had been prosecuted for knocking on doors, the Court held:

This freedom embraces the right to distribute literature… and necessarily protects the right to receive it. Martin v. City of Struthers, 319 U.S. 141, 143 (1943)17

The right to receive information is a constitutional principle that your organisation asked for and won. It did not come with an exception for information about you.

And in Watchtower Bible & Tract Society v. Village of Stratton (2002), you persuaded the Supreme Court, eight votes to one, that a town could not require canvassers to register their names before speaking — that anonymity is part of the freedom to advocate.18 Twenty-four years later you are in federal court using section 512(h) subpoenas to strip anonymity from members who write about you. You cannot have won Stratton and also believe what your subpoenas assert.

VI. What the litigation is actually for

Follow the money and it runs out immediately. You do not sell these publications. You have not sold them since 1990. There is no licensing market, no back catalogue for sale, no revenue stream that an archive could erode. Copyright exists to secure a market; here there is nothing left for it to secure.

Strip the commercial rationale away and what remains is the only purpose the enforcement can be serving: control over which of your own statements the public, and your own members, are able to read.

Consider the two halves together, because they are one act. On one side, old publications are revised, and the previous wording simply disappears — no note, no date, no record that anything moved. On the other, the people who preserve the previous wording are subpoenaed, threatened, and priced out of defending themselves. The first makes the record editable. The second makes the edits unverifiable. Neither alone would work; together they are a system.

There is a name for an organisation that revises its own history and then pursues the members who keep copies. It is not a copyright strategy. It is information control of the kind that defines a high-control religion — and the fact that it is conducted through lawyers and subpoenas rather than bonfires does not change what it is.

VII. On the changes themselves

None of this would be necessary if the revisions were made openly.

Every publisher makes mistakes. Serious ones are made by serious organisations, and correcting them is not shameful — it is the only honourable thing to do with an error. What is shameful is the method: replacing a sentence and saying nothing; letting a teaching that cost families their children quietly cease to appear; allowing a generation of members to be told that they misremembered, or that the light simply grew brighter, when in fact a specific claim was made, in print, over a specific signature, and has since been removed.

If the previous words were mistakes, say so. Say which words, in which publication, in which year, and what is now taught instead. Date the revisions. Keep the superseded editions available, as any archive of integrity does. Do it once, properly, and no site like this one would have anything left to document.

To edit the record and hope no one noticed is not correction. It is evasion — and it takes a certain kind of nerve to pursue in court the people who did notice.

The wicked flee when no one pursues, but the righteous are as bold as a lion. Proverbs 28:1

You have, over many decades, asked members to accept the loss of employment, family, community and life itself rather than compromise a principle. You have praised, in your own pages, those who would not dissemble under pressure. Apply that standard to yourselves. The organisation that taught its people to stand in a courtroom and say what they believed, whatever it cost, is now the organisation using courtrooms to make sure no one can check what it said.

Whether that is defensible is a question your own standard already answers:

Whoever lives by the truth comes into the light, so that it may be seen plainly that what they have done has been done in the sight of God. John 3:21, New International Version

Would the exemplar you point your members toward regard this litigation as coming into the light? Or as the other thing that verse describes — the works that are kept away from it?

VIII. Why the site is called Horkos

In Greek mythology Horkos is the personification of the oath, and more exactly of what befalls the one who breaks it. Hesiod names him a son of Strife, born to punish those who swear falsely. He is not a god of cosmic justice, and he does not judge. His function is narrow and unglamorous: a thing was sworn, the swearer now says otherwise, and someone should be keeping count.

The name was not chosen to be clever. Your organisation is built on an oath. Baptism is preceded by a dedication vow; members are told that what they affirm is binding, that it is made before God, and that walking it back has eternal consequences. You ask for that oath on the strength of what is printed in these publications.

An organisation that binds people by their word, and then edits its own, has invited the comparison. This site does not accuse, interpret, or demand. It records what was published, and what replaced it, and the date on which one became the other.

It keeps count. That is all it does — and if the record were being kept openly, it would not need to.
